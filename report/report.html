Predicting Shipment ETA and Delay Propagation

Aiqon take-home, revised after a code/methodology audit. The main results (Sections 2-5) are on synthetic data and do not measure real-world accuracy; Section 6 adds one narrow real-data check of port-arrival ETA only. Every number below is read from report/results.md, chain_fix_effect.md and chain_dprev_mismatch.md (generated by the code).

1. Problem framing and definitions

Delay at a milestone = actual − scheduled timestamp (hours). Final ETA = scheduled delivery + predicted final delay; a shipment is "badly delayed" if final delay > 48 h. Milestones: departure, port arrival, discharge, customs clearance, delivery. The system re-predicts at five checkpoints (booked, departed, at port, discharged, customs cleared) using only information available at that time. Missing intermediate timestamps are expected.

2. Data (synthetic) and ground truth

No free public dataset has scheduled-vs-actual times for every stage (the nearest Kaggle shipping set is itself synthetic; real port-call feeds cover one leg; carrier/AIS data is commercial), so I built a causal simulator (2,000 shipments, 10 India→Gulf/Singapore/Europe/US routes, 2023–24). Real inputs: port pairs and liner-like transit times, and real ERA5 daily wind at every port (Open-Meteo). Everything else (terminal-efficiency tiers loosely inspired by the World Bank CPPI, congestion dynamics, effect sizes, the 48 h threshold, chosen so ~55% of shipments are badly delayed) is my assumption. Propagation: dk = mk·dk−1 + ownk with mk∈[0,1] the surviving share of upstream delay (slack; at most 24 h recoverable at sea). Modelled behaviours: congestion × inefficiency interaction on berth wait and dwell; missed-berth-window jumps (+24–48 h); customs office hours (Mon–Fri 09–17) and truck dispatch cut-off; random inspections and documentation errors; real-wind weather effects; transshipment; informative missing timestamps. Each driver's hours are logged, so the final delay decomposes exactly into per-stage contributions (tested), the ground truth for scoring explanations.

3. Features, checkpoint availability and model

  • Information availability (enforced in features.py, tested): at booking only static fields (route, efficiency, cargo risk, documentation flag) are known; after departure also origin wind and the destination congestion index of the departure day; at port arrival the arrival-day congestion and wind; milestone delays only once reached. Anything unobserved is NaN, never a future value or a forecast stand-in. Feature groups: upstream cumulative delay and increments, slack vs berth window, delay × congestion, congestion × (1−efficiency), expected customs-closed and dispatch-cut-off waits from the expected clock time of the next stage, missing-stage counts.
  • Chain model (explicit propagation): per stage, a GBM predicts the stage increment from the previous stage's delay plus that stage's drivers; rolling forward from the last observed milestone gives the final delay. Direct model: one GBM over all checkpoints. Per-checkpoint blend weights.
  • Data roles: train → fit both models; calibration half A → blend weights; calibration half B → split-conformal 80% intervals and isotonic calibration; the test set is never used in fitting. Hyper-parameters were fixed a priori; the only model-side choice made after the audit (chain loss) was checked on the calibration split alone and left at the default.
  • Explanations: (a) where hours were lost: stage increment (realised, or expected for future stages) minus the typical increment; (b) indicative what-if: re-roll the chain with one stage set to normal; (c) TreeSHAP top positive driver per stage; plain-English template (no LLM).

4. Audit: what was found and fixed

  • Confirmed: chain train/inference mismatch. Stage models were trained with every exogenous input (as if at checkpoint 4) but queried at earlier checkpoints with those inputs missing. LightGBM treats NaN as 0 when training had none, so the old chain saw "zero congestion": measured, it predicted a mean port-stage increment of −0.9 h where the true mean is 13.0 h. Fix: one stage model per information regime (booked / departed / at-port-or-later), each trained on exactly the features available then. Trade-off: 3× more small models, less data per regime.
  • Confirmed: "booking" congestion was the departure-day value, not knowable at booking. Now available from the "departed" checkpoint only (renamed cong_at_departure).
  • Confirmed: calibration roles overlapped (blend weights tuned and conformal residuals computed on the same data). Now split in two halves by shipment.
  • Confirmed: weak validation (actual timeline never checked; CLI accepted incomplete records), explanation bug (driver computed with the wrong previous-stage delay for future stages; could name a negative contributor), unclosed file handles, unpinned dependencies, silent overwrite of results. All fixed; results are now archived under report/archive/ and a run-metadata file records seeds, parameters and package versions.
  • Investigated, real, fix tested and rejected: previous-stage-delay mismatch. Stage models are trained with the true previous-stage delay (teacher forcing) but, beyond the last observed milestone, receive a rolled-forward prediction. Measured on the validation split, that input has RMSE 6 / 11 / 30 / 37 h for stages 1–4 against a true standard deviation of 6 / 13 / 37 / 44 h, so far-ahead inputs are barely informative where training saw exact values. I implemented the principled fix (retrain each stage on out-of-fold rolled-forward delays, plus a flag for "previous delay observed"); on the validation split it was not better (chain-only MAE 19.96 h vs 19.3 h, worse at "discharged": 15.9 vs 13.7 h), so the default stays teacher-forced and the option (Chain(oof_folds=5)) is off. The mismatch therefore remains a known limitation of the chain model; the blend with the direct model, which has no such issue, limits its impact. Reproduce: scripts/chain_dprev_mismatch.py (validation split only).
  • Checked and found sound: temporal and route splits are isolated (tested); route hold-out excludes held-out routes from training and calibration; features contain no future timestamps (adversarial tests: altering everything after checkpoint k, including later wind/congestion and the target, leaves predictions and attributions at k unchanged; the test fails if a leak is re-introduced); bootstrap was already paired by shipment. Ablations alter only the direct model.
  • Disclosed methodological caveat: I chose the attribution accounting ("where hours were lost" rather than the counterfactual view) after seeing fidelity on the test split; this is mitigated by the replication on two fresh simulated worlds (Section 5D) that played no part in that choice.

6. Real-data check: port-arrival ETA on AIS (separate track, realworld/)

This tests one stage only: can a model correct the arrival ETA that vessels themselves declare? It does not validate customs, delivery, the five-milestone chain model or end-to-end ETA. Data: 24 consecutive days (13 Sep - 6 Oct 2026) of Danish Maritime Authority open AIS (dma.dk/safety-at-sea/navigational-information/ais-data), whose position messages carry each vessel's declared destination and ETA. A row is a (vessel, hour) state chosen with information known at that hour only; vessels that never arrive are kept. Label = min(arrival − declared ETA, 48 h), arrival = first observed entry into a circle around one of 27 ports. Split by time 60/20/20, with rows purged if their label window reaches the next period; bootstrap over (vessel, port) pairs. About 30,000 rows, ~1,100 vessel-port pairs, ~340 in test. A separate LightGBM is used here, not the synthetic-data model.

  • Modest, definition-dependent gain. LightGBM improves on the declared ETA by roughly 1.5-2 h at 10-15 km, but beats a trivial per-port median correction by at most ~1 h, and at 5 km is not distinguishable from it (its median error there, 2.03 h, is worse than the declared ETA's 1.58 h). The declared ETA is already good (median error ~1.5 h); MAE is driven by stale ETAs and no-shows. On vessels that do arrive the gain is about 1 h (3.93 → 2.87 h at 10 km). A physics-only ETA is worse than the declared one at every radius. Error is far higher on vessels unseen in training (8.67 vs 3.08 h at 10 km). 80% conformal intervals cover 0.76-0.85.
  • A first design was retracted. Keeping only calls that did arrive within a fixed window gave 12.75 h vs 24.63 h, but the declared error then scaled with the window (8 / 25 / 53 h for 48 / 96 / 168 h): conditioning on a future arrival leaks the outcome. With the current design the same check is flat (5.5 / 7.3 / 7.0 h for caps of 24 / 48 / 72 h). Checking against the data also caught two of my own bugs (a swapped pair of port codes; vessels leaving receiver coverage labelled as no-shows).
  • Limits: one region, 24 days, mixed ship types; "arrival" is a zone entry, not berthing; unknown outcomes (vessel leaves coverage) are dropped; port list, 48 h cap and tracking rule are my choices (cap and radius were varied). Details and reproduction: realworld/README.md, realworld/results.md.

7. Remaining limitations

  • All results come from a simulator I wrote with assumed parameters; the models partly learn my rules. Only wind is real. Nothing here establishes end-to-end accuracy on real shipments; the only real-data evidence is the narrow port-arrival check in Section 6.
  • Simulated congestion/weather are observed with small noise at known times; real feeds are noisier and later. Conformal intervals assume exchangeability and degrade under route shift. Ablations cover only the direct model. The what-if attribution is not validated against true counterfactuals. The 48 h threshold and delay level were set from the simulator's base rate.
  • The chain's previous-stage-delay mismatch (Section 4) is unresolved. Next steps: obtain linked shipment milestones (customs, delivery) so the propagation model itself can be tested on real data, calibrate simulator parameters on real distributions, per-route recalibration.
