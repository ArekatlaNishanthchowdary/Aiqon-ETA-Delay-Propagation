Predicting Shipment ETA and Delay Propagation

Aiqon AI internship take-home · two-page summary. Full report: report/report.pdf; secondary tables and audit log: report/appendix.md. Every number is copied from a file generated by the code.

Summary. The system predicts final ETA, the probability of a delay over 48 h, and which stage caused it, updating at five checkpoints. On synthetic shipments it cuts ETA error from 24.2 h (best baseline) to 16.0 h, and its explanations match ground truth 84% of the time once most stages are observed. On real AIS data, for the one stage that could be tested, a learned correction improves vessels' declared arrival ETA by about 1.5–2 h but barely beats a trivial baseline.

1. Problem, data and system

Delay = actual − scheduled time at a milestone; ETA = scheduled delivery + predicted final delay; badly delayed = final delay over 48 h. Predictions are made at five checkpoints (booked, departed, at port, discharged, customs cleared), each using only what is known then.

Data. No public dataset has scheduled and actual times for every shipment stage, so the main experiments use a causal simulator written for this project (2,000 shipments, ten India→Gulf / Singapore / Europe / US routes). Delay propagates as dk = mk·dk−1 + ownk: slack absorbs part of the upstream delay and each stage adds its own (congestion × terminal inefficiency, missed berth windows, customs office hours, inspections, dispatch cut-offs). Only the wind data is real; other parameters are assumptions. Each delay hour's cause is logged, giving exact ground truth for explanations.

  • No leakage: timelines are validated and a feature stays missing until its checkpoint; tests alter everything after a checkpoint and require the prediction there to be unchanged.
  • Models: a chain model (one gradient-boosted model per stage, rolled forward: explicit propagation) blended per checkpoint with a direct model.
  • Uncertainty and explanations: split-conformal 80% intervals; calibrated delay probability; hours gained or lost per stage versus a typical shipment, with the main driver and a plain-English sentence.

  • ETA: 16.0 h overall (95% CI 14.7–17.3) vs 24.2 h, a paired gain of 7.1–9.5 h; 16.9 ± 1.1 h vs 25.8 ± 2.0 h across three simulated worlds.
  • Uncertainty: 80% intervals cover 0.77–0.87 by checkpoint; delay-probability AUC 0.89 (0.82 at booking, 0.99 after customs).
  • Explanations: the most-blamed stage matches ground truth 48% of the time at booking and 84% after customs (chance 20%).
  • Weak spots: on two unseen routes error is 22.3 h (baseline 33.0 h) but coverage drops to 0.62–0.78; the blend beats the direct model alone by only 0.7–1.5 h (95% paired CI) and not in one of three simulated worlds; the chain is kept mainly for its stage-level explanations.

3. Evidence from real data

Port-arrival ETA was tested on 24 days of Danish Maritime Authority open AIS, where vessels broadcast a declared destination and ETA (about 30,000 vessel-hour rows, 27 ports; rows chosen with information known at that hour only, vessels that never arrive included; split by time). The gain is real but modest and depends on how arrival is defined; against a trivial per-port correction the model wins by at most about an hour and at 5 km is not distinguishable. An earlier design that kept only vessels which did arrive showed a far larger gain and was retracted: its error scaled with the look-back window, so the selection leaked the outcome.

Do the simulator's assumptions hold? Tested on 1,639 real port stays (direction only): heavy-tailed port times and a late tail in arrivals are supported; the assumption that congestion lengthens the port stage was not detected (correlation −0.03, CI −0.08 to +0.03, vs +0.55 in the simulator), nor was a weekend effect. The proxy is crude and the ports are small, but the simulator's strongest assumption is not externally validated.

4. Limitations

  • Synthetic data: the models partly recover rules I wrote; absolute errors do not predict real accuracy, and customs, delivery and end-to-end ETA are untested on real shipments.
  • Chain exposure bias: stage models train on the true previous-stage delay but receive a predicted one. Three corrections were tested on validation data; none was better, so the bias is measured but not removed.
  • Shift: intervals lose coverage on unseen routes; the real-data model is much weaker on unseen vessels (8.67 vs 3.08 h).
